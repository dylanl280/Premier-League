Fouling does not cost you the game. Being sent off does.

The raw figure looks damning: the side committing more fouls wins only 34.5% and loses 40.6%. Almost all of that is venue. Away sides both foul more and win less, so the headline is really measuring who was at home.

Held against each side's own base rate, the picture changes completely. Fouling more costs about two and a half points — next to nothing. A booking costs a little more. Only a sending-off genuinely decides matches, and it is the one event here that is plainly causal: the team finishes a man short.

Change in win rate against that side's base rate

The foul gradient is the tell. Committing one or two extra fouls, a team wins 35.5%; committing ten or more, 32.7%. Barely a move across the whole range — set against shots on target, which climbs from 43.8% to 92.2% over its equivalent span.

Method & caveats

Built from football-data.co.uk and a Kaggle mirror of the same source, merged and loaded into PostgreSQL. 12,734 matches, 34 seasons, 51 clubs, 164 referees. Eighteen integrity checks run on every rebuild.

Match statistics begin in 2000-01. Shots, corners, fouls, cards and referee are absent before then — 22% of matches. Goals and results are complete throughout.

Shots on target are not comparable across 2013-14. The share of shots recorded as on target falls from 56.6% to 33.2% in one summer while goals per shot barely moves — 11.1% → 10.3%. That is the source redefining the field, most likely dropping blocked shots, so every on-target figure here starts at 2013-14.

1993-94 and 1994-95 ran 42 games, not 38; the league carried 22 clubs before shrinking to 20 in 1995-96. Compare those seasons on points per game.

These are associations, not causes. Teams protecting a lead concede shots and teams chasing a game take them, so part of the shooting relationship runs backwards from the scoreline.
